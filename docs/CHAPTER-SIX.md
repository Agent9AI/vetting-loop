# Chapter Six of the Kenya Constitution — A Plain-Language Guide

**For citizens using The Vetting Loop**

---

## What Is Chapter Six?

Chapter Six of Kenya's Constitution (2010) is titled **"Leadership and Integrity."** It sets out what Kenyans have a right to expect from every person who holds public office — whether elected, appointed, or employed by the state.

The drafters of the 2010 Constitution included Chapter Six specifically because Kenya had experienced decades of public officials enriching themselves, abusing power, and facing no consequences. Chapter Six was the people's answer: a set of enforceable standards that any officer could be held against.

The central idea is straightforward: **holding public office is a public trust.** Officers are servants of the people, not the other way around.

---

## What Chapter Six Requires (Articles 73–80)

**Article 73 — Responsibilities of leadership**
Every state officer must carry out their duties in a way that brings credit to their office, is consistent with the office's purpose, and avoids any conflict between their personal interests and their public duties.

**Article 74 — Oath of office**
Every state officer must take and uphold their oath of office.

**Article 75 — Conduct of state officers**
A state officer must not maintain a bank account in a foreign country without the knowledge of a relevant authority, seek personal gain in a manner that conflicts with their duties, or be willfully misleading in the performance of their functions.

**Article 76 — Financial probity**
State officers must fully account for public money. This includes the requirement to declare any gifts, donations, or benefits received in connection with the office, and to treat any money used in error as a public trust to be repaid.

**Article 77 — Restriction on activities of state officers**
A full-time state officer may not participate in any other gainful employment. Officers must fully disclose any financial interests before or on assuming office.

**Article 78 — Disqualification from state office**
A person is disqualified from holding state office if they have been found guilty of gross violation of the Constitution or other law, or if there are serious grounds to believe they have engaged in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, corruption, or abuse of office.

**Articles 79–80 — Legislation and enforcement**
Parliament is required to enact legislation giving effect to Chapter Six. This is the **Leadership and Integrity Act (2012)** and the **Public Officer Ethics Act (2003)**, which set out disclosure requirements and enforcement mechanisms.

## The Role of the EACC

The **Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission (EACC)** is the constitutional body established under Article 79 to enforce Chapter Six. Its mandate includes:

- Receiving and verifying financial declarations from state officers
- Investigating allegations of unethical conduct
- Making recommendations on disqualification
- Public education on integrity in public life

In a vetting process, the EACC may have existing reports, investigations, or declarations on a nominee. These are among the most important source documents The Vetting Loop tracks and cites on nominee dossier pages.

## Parliament's Vetting Role

Parliament does not appoint Presidential nominees — the President does. But Parliament's role is to **vet and confirm** many of these appointments. The specific constitutional provisions vary by office:

| Office | Vetting provision |
|---|---|
| Attorney General | Article 156 |
| Inspector General of Police | Article 245 |
| Chief Justice and Deputy Chief Justice | Article 166 |
| Director of Public Prosecutions | Article 157 |
| Auditor General | Article 229 |

In each case, the relevant parliamentary committee holds a public hearing, takes submissions, and recommends approval, rejection, or deferral to the full House. The full House then votes.

Chapter Six is the constitutional standard the committee is supposed to apply. In practice, committees vary enormously in how rigorously they examine nominees against this standard. The Vetting Loop makes that variation visible.

---

## What Can Disqualify a Nominee

Under Chapter Six and the Leadership and Integrity Act, a nominee may be disqualified if they:

- Have been convicted of an offence and sentenced to more than six months imprisonment
- Have been found to have engaged in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, or corruption
- Have an undisclosed conflict of interest that is not resolved to the satisfaction of the vetting authority
- Have knowingly made a false statement in their financial disclosure
- Are listed as a director or officer of a company under investigation for fraud or financial misconduct
- Have failed to repay public funds held in error

These are not abstract standards — they are the framework behind every integrity flag on this platform.
